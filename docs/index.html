Probability: common interpretations

Classical probabilityWhen outcomes are equally likely, P(A) is the number of outcomes in A divided by the total number of possible outcomes.
Frequentist probabilityP(A) is the long-run relative frequency of event A under repeated, comparable observations.
Subjective probabilityP(A) quantifies uncertainty about A given current information and can change when new evidence arrives.

All three interpretations use the same probability rules:

\[0\leq P(A)\leq 1, \qquad P(\Omega)=1, \qquad P(A^c)=1-P(A)\]

Conditional probability: definition

Conditional probability restricts attention to cases where \(B\) has occurred.

\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B)>0\]

\(A\cap B\)
Both events occur
\(B\)
The conditioning set
\(P(A\mid B)\)
Probability of \(A\) within \(B\)

The event after the vertical bar determines the relevant denominator.

The theorem of total probability

Let \(B_1,\ldots,B_k\) form a partition of the sample space: the events do not overlap and together cover every possible outcome.

\[P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)\]

Each term combines:

\(P(A\mid B_i)\)
Probability of \(A\) within group \(i\)
\(P(B_i)\)
Frequency or prior probability of group \(i\)

The theorem produces the overall probability as a weighted average of group-specific probabilities.

Bayes’ theorem

Bayes’ theorem reverses a conditional probability by combining evidence with prior information.

\[P(B_j\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)}\]

\(P(B_j)\)
Prior probability
\(P(A\mid B_j)\)
Likelihood of the evidence
\(P(B_j\mid A)\)
Updated probability

The denominator is \(P(A)\), often obtained with the theorem of total probability.

Ecologists survey 100 ponds for an amphibian disease.

Habitat Surveyed Diseased
Forest ponds 30 12

Conditional probability
How common is disease within forest ponds?

Total probability
How common is disease across all ponds?

Teaching example with hypothetical data

Events and the habitat partition

Let \(D\) mean “disease detected.” The habitat events are:

\(F\)
forest pond
\(M\)
marsh pond
\(O\)
open pond

Every pond belongs to exactly one habitat, so \(F\), \(M\), and \(O\) form a partition of the survey.

\[P(F)+P(M)+P(O)=0.30+0.40+0.30=1\]

This partition lets us combine habitat-specific disease risks without double counting ponds.

Conditional probability

Let \(D\) mean “disease detected” and \(F\) mean “forest pond.”

\[P(D\mid F)=\frac{P(D\cap F)}{P(F)}\]

Among the 30 forest ponds, 12 have disease:

\[P(D\mid F)=\frac{12}{30}=0.40\]

The conditioning event \(F\) changes the denominator from all ponds to forest ponds.

The direction of conditioning matters

Disease given forest

\[P(D\mid F)=\frac{12}{30}=0.40\]

Select forest ponds first, then ask which have disease.

Forest given disease

\[P(F\mid D)=\frac{12}{23}=0.52\]

Select diseased ponds first, then ask which are forest ponds.

\(P(D\mid F)\) and \(P(F\mid D)\) answer different ecological questions.

The theorem of total probability

The ponds form three mutually exclusive habitat groups: forest \(F\), marsh \(M\), and open \(O\).

\[P(D)=P(D\mid F)P(F)+P(D\mid M)P(M)+P(D\mid O)P(O)\]

\[P(D)=(0.40)(0.30)+(0.20)(0.40)+(0.10)(0.30)=0.23\]

Forest contribution
0.12 of all ponds
Marsh contribution
0.08 of all ponds
Open contribution
0.03 of all ponds

Overall disease prevalence: \(0.12+0.08+0.03=0.23\)

Bayes’ theorem reverses the condition

We know disease risk by habitat. Bayes’ theorem recovers habitat composition among diseased ponds.

\[P(F\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid F)P(F)}{P(D)}\]

\[P(F\mid D)=\frac{(0.40)(0.30)}{0.23}=0.52\]

Just over half of diseased ponds are forest ponds, even though forest ponds make up only 30% of the survey.

A monitoring test can produce a surprising posterior

Let \(S\) mean “the species occupies the surveyed site” and \(+\) mean “the survey returns a positive result.” Suppose an elusive species occupies 5% of sites. A survey detects it at 80% of occupied sites and returns a false positive at 10% of unoccupied sites.

Prior occupancy\(P(S)=0.05\)
Sensitivity\(P(+\mid S)=0.80\)
False-positive rate\(P(+\mid S^c)=0.10\)

\[P(S\mid +)=\frac{(0.80)(0.05)}{(0.80)(0.05)+(0.10)(0.95)}=0.296\]

A positive result raises occupancy probability from 5% to about 30%, but does not establish presence with certainty.

Practice: invasive plant survey

Let \(I\) mean “the invasive plant occurs” and \(T\) mean “the quadrat lies beside a trail.” Twenty percent of quadrats lie beside a trail. The plant occurs in 50% of trail-side quadrats and 10% of other quadrats.

  1. What is the probability that a randomly selected quadrat contains the plant?
  2. If a quadrat contains the plant, what is the probability that it lies beside a trail?

\[P(I)=P(I\mid T)P(T)+P(I\mid T^c)P(T^c)\]

\[P(I)=(0.50)(0.20)+(0.10)(0.80)=0.18\]

\[P(T\mid I)=\frac{P(I\mid T)P(T)}{P(I)}\]

\[P(T\mid I)=\frac{(0.50)(0.20)}{0.18}=0.56\]

From Bayes’ theorem to Bayesian inference

The same probability rule can update uncertainty about an ecological parameter or model.

\[p(\theta\mid y)=\frac{p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)}\]

\(p(\theta)\)
Prior information about an ecological quantity
\(p(y\mid\theta)\)
Likelihood of the observed data
\(p(\theta\mid y)\)
Posterior uncertainty after observing the data

The event \(B\) becomes a parameter, hypothesis, or model; the event \(A\) becomes the observed ecological data.

Ellison (2004): Bayesian inference in ecology

Ellison described four central contrasts between Bayesian and frequentist inference.

Question Frequentist framing Bayesian framing
Main probability Data given a hypothesis Hypothesis or parameter given data
Meaning of probability Long-run relative frequency Degree of uncertainty given information
Earlier knowledge Not represented by a prior Expressed through a prior distribution
Model parameters Fixed but unknown Represented by probability distributions

Ellison also emphasized posterior probabilities, model uncertainty, and applications spanning population dynamics and ecosystem processes.

Source: Ellison, A. M. (2004). Bayesian inference in ecology. Ecology Letters, 7, 509–520.

Bayesian methods in ecological practice

Many methods in the ecological literature are now commonly implemented in a Bayesian framework, especially when the ecological process cannot be observed directly.

Occupancy and abundance models
Separate occurrence or abundance from imperfect detection.
State-space population models
Separate biological dynamics from observation error.
Integrated population models
Combine counts, demographic data, and other evidence.
Spatial and hierarchical models
Represent dependence across sites, years, species, or taxonomic groups.

Bayesian analysis is common for these problems, but some have frequentist alternatives. The ecological question and model structure should guide the choice.

Advantages of Bayesian methods

Direct probability statementsCredible intervals and posterior probabilities answer questions about parameters, predictions, and hypotheses directly.
Earlier evidence can be includedPriors can represent previous studies, expert knowledge, or known biological constraints.
Hierarchical structure fits ecologyVariation among sites, years, populations, and species can be modelled within one coherent analysis.
Uncertainty propagates through the modelUncertainty in latent states and parameters carries into predictions and derived quantities.
Prediction and decisions are natural outputsPosterior predictive distributions support forecasting, risk assessment, and management decisions.

Advantages come with responsibilities

Explain the prior
State its biological meaning and show how strongly it influences the result.
Check computation
Assess convergence, effective sample size, and numerical stability.
Check the model
Use posterior predictive checks and examine mismatches with the data.
Test sensitivity
Repeat the analysis with defensible alternative priors and assumptions.

Ellison’s central caution remains useful: the assumptions behind Bayesian and frequentist inference should be considered explicitly, not treated as a software choice.

Source: Ellison (2004), doi:10.1111/j.1461-0248.2004.00603.x
